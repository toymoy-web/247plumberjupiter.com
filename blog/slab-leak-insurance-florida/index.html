Does Homeowners Insurance Cover a Slab Leak in Florida?

Sometimes the damage is covered but the pipe and the concrete work often are not. A November 2024 Florida appeals ruling shows why the policy wording decides it.

    Short Answer

    What Usually Gets Paid and What Usually Does Not

    In Florida, a slab leak claim is often split into parts. A standard homeowners policy may pay for sudden damage to the house, such as soaked flooring, and it may add a narrow benefit for tearing out the part of the structure needed to reach the leak. It usually does not pay to replace the pipe itself when the pipe wore out or corroded.

    Two policy terms decide most disputes: the tear-out provision and the exclusion for repeated seepage or leakage. A long-running leak under a slab is the situation that exclusion was written for, so the details of how and when the water escaped matter a great deal.

    The Ruling

    What a 2024 Florida Appeals Court Decided

    In State Farm Florida Insurance Co. v. Feltes, decided November 27, 2024 by Florida's Sixth District Court of Appeal, a Lee County homeowner's 1961 house had its original cast iron drain line under the slab. After about sixty years it developed holes, and wastewater escaped into the compacted sand beneath the slab. A kitchen sink overflowed, then a toilet did, and the plumber said the whole line needed replacing, which meant cutting out floor and foundation.

    A jury awarded the homeowner nearly $60,000 in tear-out costs. The appeals court reversed that part. It held that the corroded pipe was excluded as wear and corrosion, that the damage to the fill under the slab was caused by repeated leakage, and that the repeated seepage exclusion applied even though water escaped only when the plumbing was used. The court said the policy does not require a steady drip, only a repeated one.

    The same opinion cites an earlier Third District case, Shotwell, which found that a toilet overflow did not trigger tear-out coverage to reach corroded pipes under a slab. Courts read the tear-out language narrowly, and it covers only the part of the structure needed to reach the point where the water escaped.

    Deadlines

    Florida's Claim Notice Window

    Under Florida Statutes 627.70132, a policyholder has one year to give notice of a new or reopened property claim and 18 months to give notice of a supplemental claim. A slow slab leak makes the clock hard to read, because the date of loss may be disputed. Reporting early protects the claim.

    Do not let a deadline decide the repair. Stopping the leak comes first, and the claim can be handled in parallel.

    Limits

    What the Ruling Does Not Say

    The decision does not say slab leaks are never covered. It turned on specific facts: an original cast iron line about sixty years old, a homeowner who conceded that wear and corrosion caused the failure, and testimony that wastewater escaped for months each time the plumbing was used. A sudden break caused by something other than age, or a burst supply line that floods the house, can raise different questions under the same kind of policy.

    A Broward County trial court ruling, reported by the insurer's law firm, reached a similar result on tear-out coverage for a leak under a kitchen slab. It found no covered damage above the slab and held that the foundation is not part of the dwelling insured under Coverage A. That is a trial court decision reported by one side, so treat it as an example, not a rule.

    Ask Your Agent

    Questions to Ask Before You Need to File

    Is there tear-out coverage?

    Ask for the exact wording and the conditions that trigger it.

    How is the seepage exclusion worded?

    Look for language about continuous, repeating, gradual, or slow leakage.

    Is there a water damage limit?

    Some Florida policies cap water damage or carry a separate deductible.

    Is drain backup covered?

    A sewer or drain backup endorsement is often optional.

    Documentation

    What a Plumbing Report Can and Cannot Do

    A good plumbing report records when and how the leak was found, the meter readings, which line failed, the pipe material and approximate age, and photos of the failure. That helps an adjuster understand what happened without anyone guessing about coverage.

    It should not argue coverage. A plumber is not an insurance expert, and a report that speculates about how long the leak ran can hurt more than it helps. Stick to what was observed and measured, and let the policy and the adjuster do the interpreting.

    Caution

    Read the Opinion and Your Own Policy

    The Feltes opinion was marked not final until the time to seek rehearing expired, so check whether it has been changed or appealed before relying on it. Policies also differ by company and by year, and some Florida policies limit or exclude slab leak coverage in different ways.

    For a specific claim, an insurance attorney or a licensed public adjuster is the right source of advice. A plumbing company can document the leak, but it cannot interpret your contract.
