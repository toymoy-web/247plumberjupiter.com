Buying a Home With Well and Septic in Jupiter Farms

Most Jupiter Farms homes have their own well and septic system. A standard home inspection does not cover them, so plan separate checks.

By the Jupiter 24/7 Plumbing team · Published October 3, 2026 · Updated October 3, 2026

    Short Answer

    Inspect the Well and Septic Separately

    A general home inspection looks at the house. It usually does not test the well pump, pressure tank, water quality, septic tank, or drain field. In Jupiter Farms, where most lots were platted in the 1950s and 1960s into acre-and-a-quarter to two-and-a-half-acre lots, those systems are part of the purchase.

    Plan for at least four checks: a septic tank inspection and pump-out, a drain field evaluation, a well and pressure tank performance test, and a water quality test. Ask each inspector to put findings in writing.

    Each system fails differently, and each has a different cost of failing.

    Septic

    Reading a Septic System

    The Florida Department of Health says a septic tank should generally be pumped every three to five years and inspected about every three years. Ask the seller for pumping records and permit paperwork. The county health department keeps permit records for septic systems, and a record that does not match the house, such as a bedroom count that is larger than the tank was sized for, is a flag.

    Septic oversight rules moved to the Florida Department of Environmental Protection in July 2021, but county health departments still handle permitting and inspection. A failing drain field is the costly problem. Wastewater surfacing on the ground, or a tank that fills quickly after pumping, are signs to investigate.

    Sewer Check

    Is Sewer Available?

    The Loxahatchee River District lists parts of Jupiter Farms in its sewer service area. Its rules require an owner to connect within one year of the sewer being declared available on a street that abuts the property, and to abandon the septic tank under state rules. Ask the District whether sewer is available or planned for the address before you buy.

    That affects the real cost of the home. A property that looks fine on septic today may face a connection cost and an abandonment cost later.

    Well Water

    Water Quality on a Private Well

    Private wells are not covered by the testing and reporting that utility customers get, so the owner is responsible. Listings in Jupiter Farms regularly mention softeners, reverse osmosis systems, and treatment for sulfur odor, which tells you what the water often needs.

    Ask for a recent test that covers bacteria, nitrates, iron, hardness, and sulfur. For a home you are buying, take your own sample, because the seller's report may be old. Treatment equipment adds cost and maintenance, so ask what is installed and its age.

    Timeline

    Add well, septic, and water-quality inspections as contingencies.

    2

    Request records

    Ask for pumping records, permits, and the age of the pump and pressure tank.

    3

    Schedule the inspections

    Have the septic pumped for inspection and the well tested before the contingency expires.

    4

    Contact the Loxahatchee River District about the address.

    5

    Use written reports to adjust price or require repairs.

    Risk

    Where the Costs Hide

    The most expensive failure in a well-and-septic home is usually the drain field, because replacing it can mean excavation, soil work, and permits. Next come well pump and pressure tank replacement, and then water treatment equipment. None of these is routine, but each can be large, and they are all easier to negotiate before closing than after.

    That is why a written inspection matters. A repair estimate attached to a report is a negotiating tool. A suspicion is not.

    Land

    Zoning, Lots, and Questions About the Land

    Most of Jupiter Farms is zoned Agricultural Residential, which allows horses and outbuildings, and the lots are large enough that irrigation sometimes draws from a pond instead of the well. Ask where the irrigation water comes from, because heavy irrigation on a well can hurt pressure and cost more to run.

    Ask also about flood zone status, easements, and the condition of the road. A paved road matters for emergency access and for the delivery trucks that pump septic tanks.

    Disclosure

    What Sellers Should Provide

    A seller can provide pumping receipts, permit records for the septic system and the well, the age of the pump and pressure tank, and any water test results. A seller who cannot find any of these has not necessarily done anything wrong, but it means more work for the buyer.

    If the well or septic system has been replaced, ask for the permit and the contractor's invoice. Those documents show what was installed and when.

    Timing

    Why Early Scheduling Matters

    Septic pump-outs and well tests take scheduling time, and the inspection window in a purchase contract is short. Book the septic inspector and the well tester as soon as the contract is signed, and ask each how soon you will receive a written report.

    If a report finds a problem, you will want time to get a repair estimate and negotiate. Waiting until the last days of the contingency leaves you little room.
